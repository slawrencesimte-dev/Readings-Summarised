Knowing What to Sell, When, and to Whom

V. Kumar, Rajkumar Venkatesan & Werner Reinartz  |  University of Connecticut & INSEAD
CRM Analytics Bayesian Estimation Purchase Prediction Customer Probability Cube Marketing ROI Overcommunication Effect

Overview & Central Problem

This article addresses one of the most persistent and costly failures in customer relationship management (CRM): despite enormous investments in data infrastructure, most companies remain remarkably bad at predicting what a specific customer will buy, which product they will choose, and — crucially — when they will make that purchase. The authors report that traditional CRM-based prediction methods achieve accuracy of only about 60%, barely better than a coin toss. This means companies routinely send the wrong offer, to the wrong customer, at the wrong time — wasting resources and, worse, actively alienating customers through over-communication.

The authors — all quantitative marketing researchers — argue the root cause is not a failure of CRM data per se, but a fundamental flaw in the mathematical methods used to analyse it. They propose a superior approach derived from Nobel Prize-winning econometric work and demonstrate that it lifts prediction accuracy to approximately 80%, with direct bottom-line impact in the hundreds of millions of dollars.

"The secret to achieving a good marketing ROI is simple: Give customers more of what they truly want and less of what they don't."

The Traditional Method and Its Fatal Flaws

Two-Step Regression Approach

The dominant industry practice splits the prediction problem into two separate, sequentially solved regression models:

  1. Product Choice Model: Estimates the probability that a customer will choose a particular product, given demographic data, past purchase history, and brand exposure variables. Output: a set of product-choice probabilities per customer.
  2. Purchase Timing Model: Estimates the probability that a customer will make a purchase in a given time period, based on average interpurchase intervals, recency, and frequency of marketing contacts. Output: timing probabilities per customer.

The joint probability — what product a customer will buy, and when — is computed by simply multiplying these two probabilities together, producing what the authors call the Customer Probability Cube (axes: Customers × Products × Time Periods).

Flaw 1: Interdependence Ignored

The timing of a purchase is intrinsically linked to the type of product being purchased. If a customer buys Product A every three months and Product B every four months, and she bought Product B two months ago, she is clearly more likely to purchase Product A right now — not Product B. But because the traditional method uses two independent regression equations, it completely ignores this interdependence between product choice and purchase timing. The resulting joint probabilities are systematically wrong.

Flaw 2: Sampling Error (The Core Problem)

More critically, all regression analysis operates under the assumption that the variable weightings (coefficients) estimated from a sample are representative of the entire customer population. This is statistically untenable. Any single sample will produce slightly different coefficients than the true population parameters — this is sampling error. When these flawed coefficients are then applied to predict behavior across hundreds of thousands of customers, the errors compound. The traditional method has no mechanism to account for or mitigate this sampling uncertainty.

The Customer Probability Cube

A three-dimensional matrix mapping each customer (rows) against each product/category (columns) and each time period (depth layers). Each cell contains the estimated probability that Customer i will purchase Product j in Quarter t. Marketers use it to identify (a) which customers to contact, (b) what to offer them, and (c) when to make the offer — enabling highly targeted, resource-efficient campaigns.

The Proposed Solution: Bayesian Estimation via a Likelihood Function

The authors build upon the pioneering econometric work of Daniel McFadden (Nobel Laureate, Economics, 2000) to develop a multivariate likelihood function that simultaneously models product choice and purchase timing as a unified, interdependent process — not two separate equations.

What is Bayesian Estimation?

Unlike regression analysis, which forces a single "best-fit" coefficient estimate per variable, Bayesian estimation works by:

  1. Specifying the full range of weightings that could have plausibly generated the observed data.
  2. Iteratively searching (via Markov Chain Monte Carlo or similar methods) through this range to find the distribution of coefficients most likely to reproduce the actual observed purchasing patterns.
  3. Making predictions that incorporate this uncertainty, rather than pretending a single set of estimates is definitively correct.

Metaphorically: regression analysis draws the best-fit line through a scatter plot; Bayesian estimation reproduces the actual scatter of dots — capturing real behavioral variability rather than suppressing it.

The Likelihood Function

The authors' model computes the likelihood (Li) that customer i will purchase a given product at a given time, accounting for:

  • The number of interpurchase intervals for that customer.
  • Whether a purchase occurred (δ = 1) or not (δ = 0) at time t.
  • Right-censoring of data (not all observation windows end at a purchase event).
  • Density and survivor functions that model the time-to-next-purchase distribution.

Implementation requires specialised statistical software (Gauss or MATLAB). The model is fitted on historical data, then the resulting parameter distributions are used to populate the Probability Cube for future periods.

The Overcommunication Finding

One of the study's most practically significant — and counterintuitive — findings concerns the relationship between marketing communication frequency and customer purchase behaviour. The authors find that this relationship is highly non-linear, taking an inverted-U shape:

  • Below a minimum threshold of contact frequency, customers lack the information or stimulus needed to purchase — so communication increases sales.
  • Above an upper threshold, customers experience communication overload and begin to disengage or develop negative attitudes toward the brand — so communication actually reduces sales.

Both companies in the study were operating above this upper threshold — i.e., they were actively destroying sales through excessive marketing contact. The new model identified the optimal contact frequency for each customer individually, allowing the companies to simultaneously reduce communication costs (31% reduction at the B2B firm, 26% at the financial services firm) while increasing revenue. This dual benefit is the key driver of the dramatic ROI improvements reported.

"Communicating too much can harm you as much as communicating too little. Many companies may be actively damaging their customer revenues in attempts to make sure that no opportunity for a sale is missed."

Key Topics & Concepts

Customer Relationship Management (CRM)

Systems that track individual customer purchase histories to enable personalised marketing. The article shows these systems are only as good as the analytical methods applied to them.

Marketing ROI

Return on investment from marketing expenditure. The core metric the article seeks to optimise by improving targeting precision and reducing wasteful communication.

Purchase Probability Estimation

Quantitative modelling of the likelihood that a specific customer will purchase a specific product at a specific time — the "triple specificity" problem that CRM systems aim to solve.

Sampling Error

The statistical inaccuracy arising when coefficients estimated on a sample are assumed to apply to the whole population. The primary flaw of regression-based CRM models.

Bayesian Estimation

A statistical approach that models uncertainty in parameter estimates by working with probability distributions of coefficients rather than single point estimates, producing more robust predictions.

Interpurchase Timing

The time intervals between successive customer purchases. A critical predictor of when a customer is "ready to buy" — and the dimension where traditional models perform worst.

Communication Threshold Effect

The non-linear relationship between marketing contact frequency and customer response, where both under- and over-communication reduce purchase rates and revenues.

Customer Segmentation via Behaviour

Using actual transactional history (purchase sequences, category patterns, timing) rather than demographics alone to segment and target customers.

Frameworks Applied

  • McFadden's Econometric Duration Analysis: The foundational statistical framework for modelling time-to-event data (here, time-to-purchase) in the presence of covariates — the basis of the authors' likelihood function.
  • Customer Lifetime Value (CLV) Logic: The broader motivation for precision targeting — every incorrect offer or ill-timed contact erodes the value of the customer relationship.
  • Test-Control Experimental Design: The field study used a classic experimental design, splitting each firm's sample into a test group (new model's contact strategy) and a control group (existing strategy) to measure causal impact.

Managerial Implications

The article argues that the tools and data already exist in most large companies — the bottleneck is analytical sophistication. Managers who adopt Bayesian likelihood-function approaches to customer prediction can expect to: (a) reduce total marketing spend while improving targeting; (b) recover sales currently lost to overcommunication; (c) generate significantly higher returns per customer contact; and (d) build stronger, less adversarial customer relationships by reducing unwanted outreach.

Marketing Myopia

Theodore Levitt  |  Professor Emeritus, Harvard Business School
Customer vs. Product Orientation Business Definition Industry Decline Self-Deceiving Growth Cycle Creative Destruction Selling vs. Marketing

Overview & Central Argument

Published originally in 1960, "Marketing Myopia" is one of the most influential articles in the history of management thought. Levitt's central and audacious claim is this: there is no such thing as a growth industry — there are only companies organised and operated to create and capitalise on growth opportunities. Industries do not decline because their markets dry up. They decline because management fails them — specifically, because managers define their business too narrowly, around the product they make rather than the customer needs they serve.

"Myopia" is the clinical term for short-sightedness. Levitt uses it metaphorically to describe the strategic near-sightedness of firms that can see their current product clearly but cannot see the broader landscape of customer needs, substitutes, and competition that surrounds it. This myopia is not born of stupidity but of very understandable organisational tendencies — tendencies that Levitt dissects with vivid historical examples and unsparing logic.

"The railroads are in trouble today not because that need was filled by others but because it was not filled by the railroads themselves. They let others take customers away from them because they assumed themselves to be in the railroad business rather than in the transportation business."

The Four Conditions of the Self-Deceiving Growth Cycle

Levitt identifies four beliefs that, when held simultaneously, guarantee that an industry will eventually enter a cycle of bountiful-seeming expansion followed by invisible, undetected decay:

  1. Population Myth: The belief that growth is assured by an expanding and more affluent population. This creates complacency — when the market grows automatically, managers do not develop the creative imagination needed to actively grow it. "If thinking is an intellectual response to a problem, the absence of a problem leads to the absence of thinking."
  2. No Substitute Myth: The belief that there is no competitive substitute for the industry's major product. This makes firms feel invulnerable and blind them to disruptive alternatives emerging from outside their industry.
  3. Mass Production Trap: Excessive faith in the strategic advantages of mass production and rapidly declining unit costs as output rises. This orients the entire organisation inward — toward production efficiency — and away from the market.
  4. R&D Obsession: Preoccupation with technically sophisticated products and laboratory excellence. Firms staffed by engineers and scientists become product-oriented by default, treating the customer as a secondary consideration.
The Self-Deceiving Cycle

Industry achieves early success → managers attribute success to the product, not the customer need → growth assumed to be automatic → investment concentrates on production and R&D → customer orientation atrophies → substitutes emerge from outside → industry declines while convinced of its own health.

The Canonical Examples

The Railroads

America's railroad industry was arguably the most celebrated growth industry of the 19th century. Yet it declined catastrophically in the 20th. Why? Not because the need for transportation declined — it grew explosively. The railroads declined because they defined themselves as being in the railroad business rather than the transportation business. They were product-oriented (steel rails, locomotives, schedules) rather than customer-oriented (helping people and goods move efficiently from origin to destination). As a result, they watched automobiles, trucks, and aircraft capture their customers without mounting any serious competitive response. They lacked, as Levitt quotes Jacques Barzun, "the will of the companies to survive and to satisfy the public by inventiveness and skill."

Hollywood and Television

The established film studios of the 1940s and 1950s defined themselves as being in the movie business. When television emerged, they treated it as a mortal threat to be resisted rather than a new channel for their true purpose: entertainment. Had they defined themselves as being in the entertainment business — customer-oriented rather than product-oriented — they would have embraced television as an expansive new opportunity. Many studios went bankrupt or through drastic restructuring. Ironically, television became a far larger business than movies ever were. Hollywood's eventual survival came not from the old moguls but from a new generation of creative talent who thought in terms of content, not medium.

The Petroleum Industry

Levitt's most extended case study is petroleum — a quintessential example of all four conditions of the self-deceiving growth cycle operating simultaneously. Oil companies defined their business as: finding crude oil → refining it → selling gasoline. They narrowly focused on improving drilling technology, refining efficiency, and marketing petrol — but never asked the deeper customer question: what do motorists actually want? The answer: frictionless personal mobility. The petrol station, Levitt observes, is "like a tax collector to whom people are compelled to pay a periodic toll" — inherently unpopular, not because of how it serves customers but because it forces them to interrupt their journeys to buy a product they cannot see, taste, or test.

The petroleum industry failed repeatedly to capitalise on its own resources: it owned natural gas but allowed independent transmission companies to build the natural gas revolution. It ignored fuel cells, battery vehicles, and solar energy — all being developed by outsiders — even while it held reservoirs of natural gas and the engineering capabilities to pursue them. The reason: protecting existing asset values and product lines blinded management to customer-serving alternatives that would have cannibalised current revenues but created vastly greater long-term value.

Other Industry Vignettes

  • Dry Cleaning: Disrupted not by better cleaning methods but by synthetic fibres that require less cleaning — a substitute from a completely different direction that dry cleaners never monitored.
  • Grocery Chains: The established corner-store grocery chains of the 1930s dismissed the first supermarkets as "cheapy" and "unethical opportunists." Those who held their ground were wiped out. The lesson: "They kept their pride but lost their shirts."
  • Electric Utilities: Assumed they had no competition. Levitt warned (in 1960!) of fuel cells, solar energy, and distributed power generation that would eventually threaten their monopoly model. The utilities' only salvation, he argued, would be to deliberately develop and deploy those alternatives themselves before outsiders did — a form of preemptive creative self-destruction.
  • Buggy Whip Manufacturers: The classic textbook example. No product improvement could save the industry once the automobile displaced the horse. Had they defined their business as "power transmission" or "energy catalyst," they might have pivoted to fan belts or automotive components. Instead, they manufactured themselves into extinction.

The Critical Distinction: Selling vs. Marketing

Levitt draws a sharp and philosophically important line between selling and marketing — a distinction that remains central to marketing education:

SellingMarketing
Focused on the needs of the sellerFocused on the needs of the buyer
Preoccupied with converting product into cashPreoccupied with satisfying customer needs through the product
Begins with the productBegins with the customer
Views marketing as a residual activity (after production)Views the entire business as a customer-creating and customer-satisfying organism
"You get rid of it; we'll worry about profits"Product is a consequence of the marketing effort

Mass production industries, Levitt argues, invert the proper relationship. They allow the logic of production efficiency to dominate strategy — resulting in firms that produce what they can make most cheaply and then pressure sales departments to push it at customers. In a truly marketing-minded firm, the product is determined by the customer's needs; production is the consequence of that determination, not its cause.

The Henry Ford Paradox

Levitt offers a brilliantly counter-intuitive reading of Henry Ford. We celebrate Ford for his production genius — the assembly line. But Levitt argues Ford's true genius was marketing. Ford started with the insight that at $500 per car, millions of Americans would buy one. He then worked backward from that price point to design a production system that could deliver it profitably. Mass production was the result of his marketing thinking, not the cause of his low prices. Ford's eventual failure — his insistence on only black cars, his unwillingness to differentiate — was a descent into product orientation, and it allowed General Motors to surpass him.

The Solution: Customer-Centric Business Definition

Levitt's prescription is sweeping and demands both conceptual and cultural transformation:

  • Broad Business Definition: Define your industry in terms of the customer need served, not the product manufactured. "What business are we in?" should be answered from the customer's perspective.
  • Customer-as-Starting-Point: An industry begins with the customer and their needs. From there, it works backward — through product design, manufacturing, raw material sourcing. This reverses the product-oriented firm's logic.
  • Creative Destruction Imperative: Industries must be willing to make their own existing products obsolete before competitors do. Oil companies must develop fuel cells. Utilities must invest in solar. Retailers must cannibalise physical stores with digital channels. The alternative is to be displaced by outsiders who have no such inhibition.
  • Leadership Responsibility: Creating a customer-oriented organisation is primarily a leadership challenge. The CEO must articulate and embody the vision that the company exists to satisfy customers — not to produce a particular product. This vision must permeate every function, every decision, every hire.
"The entire corporation must be viewed as a customer-creating and customer-satisfying organism. Management must think of itself not as producing products but as providing customer-creating value satisfactions."

Key Topics & Concepts

Marketing Myopia

Levitt's core metaphor: management's inability to see beyond the current product to the broader customer need, creating strategic blind spots that enable disruption.

Product vs. Customer Orientation

The fundamental strategic dichotomy. Product-oriented firms ask "what can we make?" Customer-oriented firms ask "what does the customer need?"

Business Definition

The strategic act of defining what industry/business a company is in. Narrow definition creates myopia; broad definition opens growth horizons.

Self-Deceiving Growth Cycle

Levitt's model of how industries decay: early success breeds complacency, complacency breeds product orientation, product orientation breeds blindness to substitutes.

Creative Destruction

Borrowed from Schumpeter — the idea that firms must proactively obsolete their own products and assets before outsiders do, even when those products are currently profitable.

Selling vs. Marketing Distinction

Selling converts product to cash (seller-centric). Marketing creates, arouses and satisfies customer needs (buyer-centric). The confusion of the two is a root cause of myopia.

Population Myth

The dangerous assumption that demographic growth automatically sustains industry growth, relieving management of the responsibility to innovate.

Production Pressures

The organisational tendency in mass-production firms to prioritise production efficiency at the expense of customer orientation — inverting the proper strategic hierarchy.

Frameworks & Theoretical Foundations

  • Market/Industry Definition Framework: The article effectively operationalises the principle that a firm's relevant market is determined by the function it serves for customers, not the technology or product it currently deploys — a cornerstone of modern competitive strategy.
  • Schumpeterian Creative Destruction: Levitt explicitly invokes this concept, arguing that firms must self-disrupt or be disrupted by others.
  • McKinsey/Ansoff Growth Logic: The article anticipates market development and diversification strategies by urging firms to see adjacent customer needs as natural growth territories.
  • Stakeholder Primacy (Customer Primacy): The article's normative claim — that the customer should be the starting point for all business strategy — prefigures much of later customer-centric management literature.

Enduring Relevance

The 2004 republication underscored that Levitt's diagnosis has only grown more urgent in a world of rapid technological disruption. The industries Levitt warned about in 1960 have largely been confirmed in his analysis: oil companies face existential threats from EVs and renewables; entertainment conglomerates were disrupted by Netflix and streaming; physical retailers have been hollowed out by Amazon. In each case, incumbents defined their business by what they produced rather than what customers ultimately valued. In a world where AI, biotechnology, and digital platforms are constantly reconstructing the boundaries between industries, the imperative to define one's business around durable customer needs — not transient product forms — has never been more consequential.

Article 3 · HBR Magazine · September 2016

The Elements of Value

Eric Almquist, John Senior & Nicolas Bloch  |  Bain & Company
30 Elements of Value Value Pyramid Maslow's Hierarchy Net Promoter Score Customer Loyalty Revenue Growth Discrete Choice Analysis

Overview & Central Argument

This article from Bain & Company researchers tackles the most fundamental question in marketing: what do customers actually value? The authors argue that while companies spend enormous energy managing the price side of the value equation, they systematically under-manage the value side — because value is perceived as too psychologically complex and context-dependent to measure rigorously. The authors challenge this assumption head-on.

Through three decades of consumer research and an ambitious empirical study of 10,000+ U.S. consumers rating nearly 50 companies, Almquist, Senior and Bloch identify 30 fundamental "elements of value" — discrete, universal building blocks of what consumers want from products and services. These elements, organised into a four-level pyramid, provide companies with a practical, actionable vocabulary for designing, evaluating, and improving customer value propositions. Critically, the research demonstrates empirically that higher scores across more elements directly and causally predict higher customer loyalty (NPS) and sustained revenue growth.

"The right combinations of value elements pay off in stronger customer loyalty, greater consumer willingness to try a particular brand, and sustained revenue growth."

The Elements of Value Pyramid

The 30 elements are organised into four categories, arranged in a pyramid where higher-order elements represent more powerful but harder-to-deliver value. The pyramid is modelled explicitly on Maslow's hierarchy of human needs, but applied specifically to consumer behaviour in product and service contexts.

Social Impact Life Changing Emotional Functional
The Elements of Value Pyramid — higher levels represent more powerful but rarer forms of value

Level 1: Functional Elements (Base)

These address practical, tangible customer needs — the most common and most expected form of value. Key functional elements include:

  • Saves time — reduces the duration of a task or process
  • Simplifies — reduces cognitive complexity
  • Makes money — enables financial gain for the customer
  • Reduces risk — decreases uncertainty or potential loss
  • Organises — helps customers manage complexity
  • Integrates — connects different products, services, or aspects of life
  • Connects — enables communication with others
  • Reduces effort — lowers physical or cognitive labour required
  • Avoids hassles — eliminates friction from processes
  • Reduces cost — lowers financial outlay
  • Quality — the baseline; excellence in product/service performance
  • Variety — breadth of choice available
  • Sensory appeal — pleasing to the senses (taste, sight, smell, etc.)
  • Informs — provides useful knowledge

Level 2: Emotional Elements

These address psychological states — how customers feel as a result of using a product or service. Emotional elements are harder to deliver reliably but generate stronger loyalty when achieved:

  • Reduces anxiety — diminishes worry or fear
  • Rewards me — provides perks, points, recognition
  • Nostalgia — evokes positive memories
  • Design / Aesthetics — visual or experiential beauty
  • Badge value — signals status to others
  • Wellness — contributes to physical or mental wellbeing
  • Therapeutic value — provides comfort or stress relief
  • Fun / Entertainment — enjoyment, pleasure, play
  • Attractiveness — makes the user feel more appealing
  • Provides access — opens doors to experiences or communities otherwise unavailable

Level 3: Life-Changing Elements

These affect customers at a deeper personal level — their sense of identity, aspiration, and belonging:

  • Provides hope — creates optimism about the future
  • Self-actualisation — enables the customer to realise their potential
  • Motivation — drives goal-directed behaviour (e.g., Fitbit)
  • Heirloom — creates lasting value for future generations
  • Affiliation & belonging — fosters a sense of community

Level 4: Social Impact (Apex)

The highest and rarest form of value — the product or service enables the customer to contribute positively to society beyond themselves:

  • Self-transcendence — enables contribution to something larger than oneself (e.g., TOMS Shoes: buy one, give one)

Research Methodology

The empirical foundation of this article is unusually strong for a practitioner piece:

  • Survey Instrument: 10,000+ U.S. consumers, each rating one company they had purchased from in the previous six months, scoring it on all 30 elements using a 0–10 scale.
  • Companies Studied: ~50 U.S.-based firms across retail, banking, insurance, brokerage, smartphones, food and beverages, and media.
  • Outcome Variables: Net Promoter Score (NPS — a proxy for customer loyalty and advocacy) and actual recent revenue growth rates.
  • Qualitative Foundation: Three decades of consumer interviews using the laddering technique — which progressively probes beneath stated preferences ("I like that my bank is convenient") to uncover underlying drivers ("I value it because it saves time, avoids hassle, and reduces effort").
  • Theoretical Grounding: Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs (1943) — extended and operationalised specifically for consumer-product contexts.

Key Empirical Findings

Finding 1: More Elements = Dramatically Higher Loyalty

Companies scoring high (8+ on a 10-point scale) on four or more elements from at least 50% of respondents achieved:

  • 3× the NPS of companies with only one high score
  • 20× the NPS of companies with no high scores

Apple, Samsung, USAA, TOMS, and Amazon exemplified this pattern. Apple — one of the strongest performers — scored highly on 11 of the 30 elements. The implication is clear: breadth of value delivery, not just depth on one dimension, is what drives advocacy and loyalty.

Finding 2: More Elements = Faster Revenue Growth

Companies with high scores on four or more elements had revenue growth four times greater than companies with only one high score. This directly contradicts the common managerial assumption that value delivery is soft and hard to link to financial performance. The data shows it is a primary driver of sustainable growth.

Finding 3: Amazon's Deliberate Value Stacking

The article examines Amazon Prime as a masterclass in deliberate, sequential value addition:

  1. 2005 launch: Reduces cost + Saves time (unlimited 2-day shipping for $79/year)
  2. Expansion: Added Provides access + Fun/entertainment (Prime Video streaming)
  3. Further expansion: Added Reduces risk (unlimited photo storage)
  4. Result: Amazon scored highly on 8 elements; Prime penetrated ~40% of U.S. retail market, enabling a price increase to $99/year — a 25% price hike justified entirely by accumulated value.

Finding 4: Digital vs. Omnichannel Value Profiles

Digital-native companies (Amazon, Netflix, Zappos) dramatically outperform traditional retailers on functional elements — particularly saves time, avoids hassles, and reduces cost. Netflix scored 3× higher than traditional TV providers on reduces cost, therapeutic value, and nostalgia. Zappos scored 2× higher than apparel competitors on saves time and avoids hassles.

However, brick-and-mortar retailers retain meaningful advantages on emotional and life-changing elements — particularly badge value, attractiveness, and affiliation & belonging. Companies that receive in-store assistance score significantly higher on emotional elements. This explains why some physical retailers survive and thrive despite digital competition: human interaction delivers emotional value that digital-only channels cannot replicate.

Finding 5: The Universal Primacy of Quality

Across all industries studied, perceived quality is the single most powerful predictor of customer advocacy. It is a necessary but not sufficient condition: no amount of excellence on other elements can compensate for a significant quality shortfall. Quality is the entry ticket; everything else is the competitive differentiation.

Putting the Elements to Work: The Four-Stage Process

The article presents a structured process for using the elements framework to design new value propositions — illustrated with a detailed case study of a financial services company seeking to attract more retail banking customers:

  1. Structured Listening: In-depth individual and group interviews with current and prospective customers to map their priorities, frustrations, and trade-offs. Goal: understand the lived experience of being a customer.
  2. Ideation Sessions: Cross-functional workshops (not just marketing — all customer-facing departments) using the 30 elements as a creative stimulus. Teams explore which elements are most relevant to their target customers and which are feasible to deliver at reasonable cost. In the bank case study, this produced 12 distinct product concepts built around reduces cost, makes money, and reduces anxiety.
  3. Customer-Centric Prototype Design: Each concept tested in rapid one-on-one consumer interviews to gather fast feedback and narrow the field. The bank's 12 prototypes were winnowed to four.
  4. Rigorous Choice Modelling (Discrete Choice Analysis): The four finalist prototypes are tested with thousands of customers in a structured experiment. Respondents are asked to choose between sets of options that vary systematically on key attributes (fees, features, service levels, etc.). Statistical analysis of these choices reveals the winning combination of attributes — a consumer-validated product design.

Practical Applications of the Framework

  • New Product Development: Use the 30 elements as a structured checklist for identifying unmet value opportunities. "Can we connect with consumers in a new way? Can we add therapeutic value? Can we reduce anxiety in a category where it is currently high?"
  • Pricing Strategy: Before raising prices, assess whether sufficient additional value elements have been added to justify the higher price in the consumer's perception — as Amazon did incrementally with Prime.
  • Competitive Positioning: Map where your scores fall versus competitors' on the elements most important to your category. Address gaps in critical elements before adding new ones.
  • Customer Segmentation: Different demographic and behavioural segments weight elements differently. Segmentation by value priorities (rather than just demographics) enables more precise product design and marketing.
  • Organisational Design: Assign someone explicit responsibility for monitoring and managing value delivery across all 30 elements. The article cites a cable TV executive lamenting: "I don't have anyone really thinking about consumer value elements in a holistic manner."

Connection to Maslow's Hierarchy

The article explicitly situates its framework within the tradition of Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs (1943). Maslow argued that human motivation arises from a hierarchy of needs ranging from physiological (food, warmth, safety) through social (belonging, esteem) to self-actualisation and transcendence. The conventional interpretation holds that lower needs must be met before higher needs become motivating — though Maslow himself acknowledged more nuanced, simultaneous patterns.

The Elements of Value pyramid is an explicit application of this logic to consumer behaviour: functional needs (saving time, reducing cost, providing quality) must be met first; emotional and life-changing elements become powerful differentiators once the functional baseline is established; and social impact sits at the apex as the most rare and powerful form of value. Like Maslow, the authors acknowledge that successful products and services often deliver elements from multiple levels simultaneously — there is no rigid sequential progression.

Hierarchical model of value, analogous to Maslow's needs hierarchy, where higher-order elements generate stronger loyalty but are harder to deliver.

A customer loyalty metric based on the question "How likely are you to recommend this company?" Used as the primary measure of customer advocacy in the study.

A qualitative interviewing method that probes beneath stated preferences to uncover underlying psychological motivations — the methodological foundation of this research.

A quantitative market research technique in which customers choose between hypothetical product configurations that vary on specified attributes, revealing the relative importance of each attribute.

The 1943 psychological theory of human motivation that provides the theoretical architecture for the elements of value pyramid.

Perceived Quality

The foundational, non-substitutable element of value — the single element most predictive of customer advocacy across all industries studied.

The strategic practice of deliberately and sequentially adding new elements of value to a core offering over time — exemplified by Amazon Prime's evolution.

Digital vs. Omnichannel Value

The empirically demonstrated pattern that digital firms excel on functional elements (saves time, avoids hassle) while physical retailers retain advantages on emotional and social elements.

Customer Advocacy

The degree to which customers actively recommend a brand — the behavioural outcome most strongly predicted by high scores across multiple value elements.

Frameworks Applied

  • Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs: The conceptual foundation — extended from general human psychology to consumer behaviour in commercial contexts.
  • Net Promoter Score (NPS) Framework (Reichheld): Used as the empirical measure of customer loyalty to validate the model's predictive power.
  • Conjoint/Discrete Choice Analysis: The quantitative methodology used to test prototype concepts and identify winning product configurations from consumer choices.
  • Customer Value Proposition Design: The broader strategic framework into which the elements sit — identifying what value you deliver, to whom, and how it differentiates you from competitors.
  • Jobs-to-Be-Done Theory (Christensen): Complementary framework; where Christensen asks "what job is the customer hiring this product to do?", the elements of value provide a structured taxonomy for answering that question at scale.

Significance for Consumer Behaviour

For students of consumer behaviour, this article is significant on multiple levels. First, it bridges the gap between academic consumer psychology (Maslow, motivation theory, the psychology of needs) and managerial practice (product design, pricing, segmentation). Second, it demonstrates that consumer value is not infinitely idiosyncratic — while individual preferences vary, there is a finite set of underlying value dimensions that recur across consumers and contexts. Third, it provides a structured vocabulary for what is often treated as an intuitive or creative process: understanding and engineering consumer value. Finally, its empirical linkage between value delivery and business outcomes (loyalty, advocacy, revenue growth) establishes that consumer behaviour insights have direct and measurable strategic and financial consequences — bridging the gap between behavioural science and corporate performance.
